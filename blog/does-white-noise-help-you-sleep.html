Does White Noise Actually Help You Sleep? What the Research Says

By Health Camper · October 2026 · 6 min read

The neighbour's door. A car alarm three streets away. The fridge that only hums when you're almost asleep. Most of us don't wake up because of the noise itself — we wake up because the noise changed.

That's the whole idea behind white noise, and it's also why the honest answer to "does it work?" is: it depends on where you sleep.

How white noise works: masking, not magic

White noise is a steady "shhh" that contains all audible frequencies at once. It doesn't sedate you. What it does is raise the background level of sound in your room, so a sudden noise stands out less against it. A door slam in a silent room is a jolt; the same slam over a gentle wash of sound is just a bump. Sleep researchers call this masking.

What the studies say

In noisy places, the results are encouraging. In a 2021 study of New Yorkers who slept in high-noise environments, using a white-noise device for a week meant people reported falling asleep faster, and wrist-worn trackers recorded less time awake during the night (Ebben et al., Sleep Medicine, 2021). Similar benefits show up in hospital settings, where alarms and footsteps never stop.

Overall, the evidence is still thin. A 2021 systematic review of 38 studies in Sleep Medicine Reviews (Riedy et al.) found the quality of evidence for continuous noise as a sleep aid to be weak, with mixed results — some people slept better, a few slept worse. The studies were small and very different from each other.

So the fair summary: white noise is a helpful tool if noise is what's waking you. If your room is already quiet, it may not add much — and that's fine.

Who is most likely to benefit

You're a good candidate if you live on a busy street, share a wall, work night shifts and sleep during the day, travel a lot and sleep in unfamiliar rooms, or share a bed with a snorer. You're also a candidate if silence itself feels loud — some people find a steady sound gives a racing mind something neutral to rest on.

How to try it tonight

Keep it quiet — just loud enough to soften the room, roughly the level of a soft shower in another room. Put the phone or speaker across the room, not next to your ear. Pick a sound you'd happily hear for hours: pure white noise is bright; rain or "brown" noise is deeper and softer. And give it a few nights — the first night with any new sound can feel strange.

"You don't need silence to sleep. You need nothing to suddenly change."

Everyday wellbeing information, not medical advice. If sleep problems last most nights for more than a month, or you snore loudly or stop breathing at night, please talk to a doctor.
